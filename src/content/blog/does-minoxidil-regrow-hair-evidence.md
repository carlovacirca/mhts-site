Minoxidil can regrow hair for a meaningful number of men with pattern hair loss, but it is not a cure, it takes months to show anything, and whatever it gives back is rented rather than owned. Stop using it and the benefit fades over a few months. That is the honest shape of the evidence, whether you use the drops, the foam, or the low-dose tablet.

This article works through what minoxidil actually does in the scalp, what a 2024 head-to-head trial found when oral and topical versions were tested against each other, realistic timelines, who tends to do well, the side effects worth knowing about, and what a non-drug route looks like if daily medication isn't for you.

## How does minoxidil actually work on hair?

Minoxidil started life as a blood pressure drug. It is a vasodilator, meaning it widens blood vessels, and that is still its approved use in tablet form at higher doses for hypertension that hasn't responded to other medications, as [DermNet's minoxidil guidance](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/minoxidil-solution) explains.

Its effect on hair growth is a separate story, and one that is still only partly understood. According to DermNet, proposed mechanisms include promoting hair cell survival, prolonging the active growing phase of the hair cycle while shortening the resting phase, stimulating cell proliferation during growth, and opening potassium channels in blood vessel walls to improve blood flow to the follicle. None of this is settled science down to the mechanism, which is a fair reflection of where dermatology actually is with this drug after decades of use.

What matters for you day to day is simpler: minoxidil is applied or taken to try to nudge more follicles into an active growing state and keep them there longer. It does not change the genetic sensitivity that caused the hair loss in the first place.

## Topical drops, foam, or a daily tablet: what's the difference?

There are two distinct ways people use minoxidil for hair loss, and they are not interchangeable in how they're regulated.

**Topical minoxidil** is approved for treating male and female pattern hair loss in adults aged 18 and over, and is widely available over the counter or as a pharmacy medicine, as [DermNet explains](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/minoxidil-solution). It comes as a 2% or 5% liquid, or a 5% foam, and the standard regimen is twice-daily application, morning and night.

**Low-dose oral minoxidil (LDOM)** is a different proposition. The tablet form is licensed for hypertension, not hair loss, so using it for hair is off-label. Based on an international consensus of hair specialists, [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/minoxidil-solution) gives suggested off-label doses of 1.25 mg to 5 mg once daily for adult men. Because it's a systemic medication rather than something applied locally, DermNet recommends reviewing a person's cardiac and blood pressure history before starting, and ongoing checks of blood pressure, weight and pulse at follow-up visits. This is prescribing territory, not a self-started pharmacy purchase.

## What does the head-to-head trial actually show?

Until recently, most of what we knew about oral minoxidil for hair loss came from case series rather than trials that directly compared it with the topical version. That changed with a 2024 randomised clinical trial published in [JAMA Dermatology](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamadermatology/fullarticle/2817326), which set out specifically to compare the efficacy, safety and tolerability of daily oral minoxidil against twice-daily topical minoxidil in men with androgenetic alopecia over 24 weeks.

The trial was double-blind and placebo-controlled, run at a single clinic in Brazil, enrolling men aged 18 to 55 with Norwood-Hamilton pattern hair loss. As a companion summary in [JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2818445) put it, the results were based on data from 68 men, and the researchers found no difference in hair density between the oral and topical groups after treatment.

Side effects differed by route rather than by severity. The same summary notes that adverse effects from either drug were mild overall, but more men in the oral group experienced excessive body hair growth and reported headaches, while more men in the topical group experienced itching and eczema at the application site.

This lines up with DermNet's own reading of the broader evidence: early studies suggest both treatments are similarly effective, with no significant difference in hair density or diameter, and that treatment success will likely depend on patient adherence rather than which form is chosen, according to [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/minoxidil-solution). In plain terms, the real question is not which route is biologically superior, it is which one you will actually keep using, correctly, for a year or more.

## What results can you realistically expect, and how long does it take?

Patience is built into this treatment. DermNet states that the increased hair growth associated with minoxidil typically becomes noticeable only after four to six months of consistent use, and that the full benefit may only be seen after a year of consistent use, as set out in [DermNet's guidance](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/minoxidil-solution).

That timeline applies whether you're using drops, foam or tablets. There is no version of minoxidil that works in weeks. If you start and judge it after six weeks, you are judging it too early either way.

It is also worth being clear about what counts as a result here. Minoxidil is described by DermNet as not a cure for hair loss. It can help some follicles grow more and for longer, but it does not reverse the underlying genetic process driving pattern hair loss, and it will not restore a fully bald area to the density it had in your twenties. Anyone promising that outright is overstating what the evidence supports.

## Who tends to respond, and who should be cautious?

Minoxidil is not a treatment to start blind. [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/minoxidil-solution) makes the point that treatment with minoxidil before a proper diagnosis may mask the presence of co-existing forms of alopecia, and that the specific type or types of hair loss should be identified before starting. Diffuse pattern thinning, sudden patchy loss, and scarring forms of alopecia are not the same condition, and they don't all respond the same way.

For people whose hair loss is confirmed as genetic, DermNet notes that certain types may respond well to combination treatments, including anti-androgen therapy such as finasteride or spironolactone. If you're weighing that option, our separate article on the [2026 finasteride safety warning](/blog/finasteride-mhra-safety-warning-2026) covers what the MHRA has said and what to discuss with a prescriber before combining treatments.

There are also groups who should be cautious or avoid minoxidil altogether. On the topical side, DermNet lists contraindications including hypersensitivity to the product, hair loss that doesn't match the pattern the product is intended for, sudden or patchy shedding, broken or inflamed scalp skin, and pregnancy or breastfeeding, where data is inadequate. For low-dose oral minoxidil, the list is longer and more serious: contraindications include pulmonary hypertension with mitral stenosis, congestive heart failure, angina, a recent heart attack, pericarditis or pericardial effusion, phaeochromocytoma, severe liver impairment, and pregnancy or breastfeeding. This is exactly why oral use needs a prescriber who reviews your cardiac history, not a decision made alone, as [DermNet's precautions section](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/minoxidil-solution) sets out.

## What are the side effects, and when should you get medical advice?

Side effects differ by route, and knowing them in advance means you'll recognise what's expected versus what needs a call to your GP.

With **topical minoxidil**, [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/minoxidil-solution) lists:

- Local itching or irritation at the application site
- Allergic contact dermatitis to minoxidil or to propylene glycol, an ingredient in some solutions
- A temporary shedding phase lasting four to six weeks as the hair cycle adjusts
- Unwanted hair growth close to where it's applied

With **low-dose oral minoxidil**, the list is broader and includes effects related to its blood-vessel-widening action. DermNet describes vasodilatory effects such as lightheadedness, low blood pressure and a fast heart rate, usually apparent within the first week of treatment, fluid retention in the first four to six weeks presenting as swollen feet or hands, puffiness around the eyes, unexpected weight gain, chest pain or shortness of breath, unwanted facial and body hair typically appearing by months two to four, plus rash, headache and insomnia. DermNet also notes rare but serious reactions including pericardial effusion, cardiac tamponade, and worsening of existing heart failure or angina, all detailed in its [side effects section](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/minoxidil-solution).

If you're on oral minoxidil and notice chest pain, shortness of breath, or sudden swelling, that is not something to wait out. Contact your GP promptly, or call 999 if symptoms feel severe or sudden. For anything less urgent but still concerning, a conversation with your GP or the prescriber who started you on treatment is the right next step, not a forum post or a guess.

## What happens if you stop?

This is the part people are often not told clearly enough before they start. [DermNet states plainly](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/minoxidil-solution) that if treatment is discontinued, the positive effects are lost over a three to six month period. More specifically, in cases where treatment for androgenetic alopecia is stopped after several years, the hair that was genetically programmed to be lost during that time will be shed.

In other words, minoxidil doesn't bank progress. It holds a line for as long as you keep using it. Stop, and the hair loss that was being held off catches up with you over a few months, roughly returning you to where you would have been without treatment. That's a legitimate reason some men decide daily medication, indefinitely, isn't the route they want to commit to.
