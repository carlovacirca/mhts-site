Yes, in some men it can. A GP practice page on [hair loss and alopecia](https://bangholmmedicalcentre.scot.nhs.uk/hair-loss-alopecia) lists nutritional deficiencies directly alongside genetics and thyroid problems as a recognized cause, noting that a 
lack of essential nutrients like iron and vitamin D can contribute to hair loss.
 That does not mean every thinning patch is a diet problem. It means it is worth ruling out before you spend time or money on anything cosmetic.

This article sets out what the evidence actually supports, what a GP will check first, and where a hair density treatment or hair system fits in once you know what you are dealing with.

## Can a deficiency really cause your hair to thin?

It can contribute, and it sits on a list with other genuine medical causes rather than standing alone as folklore. The same [GP practice page on hair loss](https://bangholmmedicalcentre.scot.nhs.uk/hair-loss-alopecia) sets out the common causes plainly: 
genetic causes such as male and female pattern baldness are often inherited, medical conditions including thyroid disorders and anaemia can cause hair loss, severe physical or emotional stress can lead to temporary hair loss, and a lack of essential nutrients like iron and vitamin D can contribute
.

Notice the word used there is "contribute", not "cause on its own" in every case. That distinction matters. A deficiency rarely acts alone. It tends to show up alongside stress, illness, a restrictive diet, or a thyroid problem that is already affecting your hair cycle, which is exactly why doctors investigate several things at once rather than jumping straight to one answer.

## What does the NHS actually tell GPs to check?

For diffuse hair loss, which means thinning spread fairly evenly across the scalp rather than a patch or a receding pattern, the clinical guidance used across NHS Scotland dermatology services is specific. The [NHS Scotland alopecia pathway](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/dermatology-pathways/alopecia/) sets out, under clinical tips for diffuse non-scarring alopecia: 
"Diffuse alopecia: check full blood count, ferritin and thyroid function."


That single line does a lot of work. A full blood count picks up anaemia and several other blood disorders. Ferritin is the standard marker for your iron stores, and it is checked separately from a basic blood count because iron can be running low before anaemia shows up on anything else. Thyroid function covers both an underactive and an overactive thyroid, both of which are established causes of hair shedding.

The same [NHS Scotland guidance](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/dermatology-pathways/alopecia/) also separates out what diffuse thinning typically looks like in practice. Under diffuse non-scarring alopecia it lists the physiological, age related process, 
female pattern hair loss showing as thinning over the crown
, and 
telogen effluvium, described as excessive shedding occurring in the months after pregnancy or another major event
. A deficiency fits most naturally into that last category. It is a trigger that pushes hair into a resting phase, and the shedding tends to show up some time after the deficiency actually started, not immediately.

## Iron and hair: what is the actual connection?

Iron deficiency is one of the two nutrients named specifically in the GP guidance above, alongside vitamin D. The mechanism doctors investigate is straightforward: your body needs iron to make the proteins involved in cell growth and repair, and hair follicles are some of the fastest dividing cells in the body, so they are sensitive to a shortfall.

This is exactly why ferritin is checked as its own blood test rather than being left to a basic full blood count, under the same [NHS Scotland dermatology guidance](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/dermatology-pathways/alopecia/). Ferritin can drop before you become clinically anaemic, and hair shedding can start at that earlier stage. It is also why a GP will often ask about diet, heavy periods, blood donation, or digestive conditions that affect absorption, because the test result on its own does not explain why your iron is low, and the underlying reason is what actually needs treating.

If a test shows low ferritin, your GP will talk you through next steps, which usually means dietary advice, supplementation, or further investigation into why the level dropped in the first place. None of that is something a hair studio can or should advise on. It belongs with your GP.

## Vitamin D and other nutrients: what should you actually expect?

Vitamin D sits on the same list as iron in the [GP guidance on hair loss](https://bangholmmedicalcentre.scot.nhs.uk/hair-loss-alopecia) already cited. Evidence connecting vitamin D specifically to hair loss is less settled than the evidence for iron, and GPs typically only test for it where there are risk factors or other symptoms present, not as a routine first step for every thinning complaint. If your GP does check it and finds a shortfall, correcting it follows the same ordinary NHS supplementation approach used for vitamin D deficiency generally, and it is worth discussing timescales and expectations directly with them rather than guessing from a label.

Beyond iron and vitamin D, the same [hair loss guidance](https://bangholmmedicalcentre.scot.nhs.uk/hair-loss-alopecia) flags 
stress, certain medications, and other illnesses
 as causes worth considering in the same conversation, because several of these often overlap. A period of poor appetite during a stressful stretch, for example, can affect both stress hormones and nutrient intake at the same time, which is one more reason a blood test beats trying to self diagnose from symptoms alone.

One practical, low risk piece of general advice from the same source is worth repeating here: 
improving diet, managing stress, and avoiding harsh hair treatments can help reduce hair loss
 as a lifestyle measure. That is sensible for most people regardless of what a blood test shows, but it is support for your hair's general condition, not a replacement for addressing a confirmed deficiency or thyroid problem.

## What if your bloods come back normal?

This happens often, and it is useful information rather than a dead end. If your full blood count, ferritin and thyroid function are all within range, the [NHS Scotland pathway](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/dermatology-pathways/alopecia/) points toward the other categories of diffuse thinning: the physiological, age related kind, or a pattern related thinning over the crown. Neither of those is caused by diet, and neither is something a supplement will change.

It is also worth knowing that the same [NHS Scotland pathway](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/dermatology-pathways/alopecia/) sets out a separate route for genuinely severe, disease related hair loss, where a 
hairpiece or wig prescription is available for alopecia caused by trauma or disease resulting in psychological distress
, subject to local availability. That is a specific clinical pathway for a specific situation, assessed by a dermatologist. It is different from choosing a private, cosmetic hair system for ordinary pattern thinning, which is the situation most of our clients are actually in, and it is worth not confusing the two when you are reading about options.

If your bloods are clear and what you are looking at is straightforward diffuse thinning or an established pattern, that is the point where a private, cosmetic option becomes relevant, because you are no longer looking for a medical explanation. You already have one, and now you are deciding what to do about how your hair looks.

Do not skip the blood test. It is a small amount of effort for a genuinely useful answer, and it is free through your GP. If a deficiency or a thyroid issue is found, treating that comes first, properly, under medical supervision, before anything cosmetic is worth considering.
