Yes. Scalp micropigmentation is legally classed as semi-permanent skin colouring, which puts it under the same local authority framework as tattooing and cosmetic piercing. In Buckinghamshire that means both the person doing your treatment and the premises they work from must be registered with Buckinghamshire Council, and the room has to meet specific hygiene byelaws. There is no single national SMP licence that covers all of this for you, which is exactly why it is worth checking before you book rather than after.

This is not a technicality. It is the difference between a needle that has only ever touched you and one that has not, and between a dye that is sterile and one that is not. Here is what the law actually requires, what Buckinghamshire's own byelaws say happens in the room, and what the profession's own standards body recommends you check before you commit.

## Is scalp micropigmentation actually regulated, or is it a grey area?

It is regulated, but through a route that surprises a lot of people. SMP is not covered by a bespoke beauty or medical licence. Instead it falls under legislation written for tattooing. Under the Local Government (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1982, as amended in 2003, local authorities are responsible for regulating and monitoring businesses offering permanent tattooing and semi-permanent skin colouring, as set out in BAHRS's own [Professional Standards for Scalp Micropigmentation Practitioners](https://www.bahrs.co.uk/content/large/documents/bahrs_standards/pssmppv1march19.pdf).

That single phrase, semi-permanent skin colouring, is how SMP is captured by the law. Buckinghamshire Council's own byelaws confirm this directly, describing rules made for securing hygiene in premises and of persons registered in connection with the business of tattooing, semi-permanent skin-colouring, cosmetic piercing or electrolysis, among other practices covered by the same framework.

The important word in both of those is local. This is not a central government licence issued once and valid everywhere. It is administered council by council, which is part of why standards and paperwork can look different if you compare a studio in Buckinghamshire with one in a neighbouring authority.

## What actually has to be registered, the person or the building?

Both, separately, and it is worth understanding why.

Buckinghamshire's byelaws are built around two registration routes in the 1982 Act. One covers people, registered under sections 14(1) or 15(1), and the other covers premises, registered under sections 14(2) or 15(2). The byelaws themselves state that they exist for securing the cleanliness of premises registered under those sections and fittings in such premises and of persons registered under those same sections and persons assisting them, which is a formal way of saying the room and the practitioner are each checked on their own terms.

In practice that means a qualified SMP practitioner cannot simply rent a chair in an unregistered room and call it compliant. The room has to be registered too, and it has to meet the physical hygiene standards set out below. If either half is missing, something is wrong.

## What do Buckinghamshire's hygiene byelaws actually require in the treatment room?

This is the part that is easy to skip past, but it is the most concrete evidence of whether a studio is taking this seriously.

Under the byelaws, a proprietor has to ensure a number of specific things happen every time someone is treated:

- Any needle used in treatment is single-use and disposable, as far as is practicable, or otherwise is sterilized for each treatment, is suitably stored after treatment and is disposed of in accordance with relevant legislation and guidance as advised by the local authority.
- Any dye used for tattooing or semi-permanent skin colouring is sterile and inert, and any container used to hold it is either disposed of at the end of each treatment or cleaned and sterilized before reuse.
- Operators wear disposable examination gloves that have not previously been used with another client.
- Any table, couch or item of furniture used in treatment is covered by a disposable paper sheet which is changed for each client.
- Any surface a needle or instrument is placed on has a smooth, impervious finish that is disinfected immediately after use and at the end of each working day.
- No eating, drinking or smoking is permitted in the treatment area, with notices to that effect displayed.

None of this is unusual or excessive. It is the baseline the council expects, and it is the baseline you should expect too. If you are being treated somewhere that cannot show a dedicated treatment area, a new pack of needles opened in front of you, and a fresh paper sheet on the couch, something in that chain has been skipped.

You can read the [full byelaws for tattoo and skin piercing treatments](https://www.buckinghamshire.gov.uk/business/business-licences-and-permits/tattoo-and-skin-piercing-treatments/register-to-perform-tattoo-skin-piercing-treatments/byelaws-for-tattoo-and-skin-piercing-treatments/) directly on the council's website if you want to see the full wording.

## Why isn't there one single national licence for SMP?

Because SMP grew up inside legislation that was never written with it in mind, and the professional body that now represents practitioners is upfront about the gap this leaves.

BAHRS, the British Association of Hair Restoration Surgery, took on scalp micropigmentation practitioners as members from 2013, and it did not publish its own [professional standards document](https://www.bahrs.co.uk/content/large/documents/bahrs_standards/pssmppv1march19.pdf) until March 2019. Before that point, there were also no Professional Standards for Scalp Micropigmentation in the UK and no Code of Conduct for Scalp Micropigmentation Practitioners.

Even with that document in place, BAHRS is clear that licensing is not uniform. SMP practitioners' licensing requirements in the UK vary from council to council, although all practitioners and any premises they work from must be fully licensed by the relevant local authority. One council might ask for an annually renewable special treatment licence, another a one-off payment, with different fee structures and conditions attached. What stays constant is that it is a criminal offence to trade without registration or in breach of the byelaws, wherever you are.

This matters for you as a reader because it means the burden of checking does not sit with a national regulator doing it for you. It sits, in part, with you.

## What does BAHRS recommend you check before you book?

BAHRS's [professional standards for SMP practitioners](https://www.bahrs.co.uk/content/large/documents/bahrs_standards/pssmppv1march19.pdf) set out a fairly detailed list of what a properly operating practitioner should be able to demonstrate. It is a useful checklist regardless of which studio you are considering, in Buckinghamshire or anywhere else.

On qualifications and training, BAHRS defines a Scalp Micropigmentation Practitioner as someone who will hold an awarding organization (AO) accredited Level 4 qualification specifically in Scalp Micropigmentation (SMP), which it says separates them from practitioners trained only in other forms of micropigmentation, or in tattooing generally. Alongside that qualification, BAHRS expects practitioners to have completed a blood-borne pathogen course or equivalent, hold a first aid certificate, and hold a certificate in basic life support.

On safe practice day to day, the standards state that a practitioner should use sterile needles with a cartridge system, and should record the needle batch number and expiry date on the treatment record for every client. Dye and pigment should be sterile and inert, with safety data sheets available in the studio.

On paperwork that protects you if something goes wrong, BAHRS expects practitioners to carry adequate insurance, which it describes as entailing malpractice and clinic insurance as well as personal liability, and to hold Disclosure and Barring Service clearance, previously the remit of the Criminal Records Bureau. It also expects records of required immunization, including Hepatitis B vaccination, given the blood-borne virus risk that any skin-penetrating procedure carries.

On the physical setup, the standards call for treatment to take place in a dedicated treatment room with a handwash facility and a non-permeable floor covering, a clinical waste contract for safe disposal, and electrical equipment that is PAT tested annually.

## What questions should you actually ask a studio before you book?

Taking all of the above, here is a short, practical list worth working through, whether you are comparing studios in Amersham, High Wycombe, Rickmansworth or anywhere else in the area.

1. Can I see the practitioner's current local authority registration, and is the premises separately registered too?
2. What is your SMP qualification, and is it a Level 4 accredited specifically in scalp micropigmentation?
3. Are your needles single-use and sterile, and can you show me the sealed pack before treatment?
4. What insurance do you carry, and does it cover scalp micropigmentation specifically rather than general micropigmentation or tattooing?
5. Have you completed blood-borne pathogen training, and do you hold a current first aid certificate?
6. Is a patch test offered, and what does aftercare look like if something reacts unexpectedly?

None of these questions are awkward to ask a studio that is operating properly. A reasonable practitioner will expect them and answer plainly.

## What if a studio cannot answer these questions clearly?

Treat that as useful information in itself. Under all current legislation it is a criminal offence to trade without registration (licensing) or to be in breach of the relevant byelaws. A studio that is vague about its registration, cannot show you a sealed needle pack, or waves off questions about insurance and training is not a studio worth trusting with a procedure that is designed to last for years.

This is also not purely a medical question and we are not going to position it as one. SMP is a cosmetic procedure, not a treatment with clinical risk in the way a prescribed medicine carries, but it does involve breaking the skin, which is precisely why the hygiene byelaws exist. If you ever have concerns about a reaction after treatment, the right step is your GP or a dermatologist, not a forum post.
