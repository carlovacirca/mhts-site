Yes, it is genuinely worse, not just in your head. Dandruff and seborrhoeic dermatitis follow a real seasonal pattern, and both the [British Association of Dermatologists](https://cdn.bad.org.uk/uploads/2021/12/29200252/Seborrhoeic-Dermatitis-Update-April-2018-Lay-reviewed-April-2018.pdf) and [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/seborrhoeic-dermatitis) record flare ups in cold weather that settle again once the weather warms. If your scalp has gone from fine in September to flaky, tight or itchy now the heating is on, that fits a well documented pattern rather than anything you are doing wrong.

For anyone wearing a hair system, scalp comfort is not a side issue. The base sits directly against that skin for weeks at a time, so a scalp that is flaking, sore or inflamed affects how the system feels day to day. Here is what is actually happening, and what to do about it.

## Does cold weather really make dandruff and scalp irritation worse?

It does, and this is not a myth repeated by shampoo brands. The [British Association of Dermatologists](https://cdn.bad.org.uk/uploads/2021/12/29200252/Seborrhoeic-Dermatitis-Update-April-2018-Lay-reviewed-April-2018.pdf) states plainly that 
it is more common in cold than in warm weather, and it is not related to diet
. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dandruff/) gives the same picture for straightforward dandruff, noting that 
stress and cold weather may also make dandruff worse
.

[DermNet's](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/seborrhoeic-dermatitis) clinical description of adult seborrhoeic dermatitis lists winter flares, improving in summer following sun exposure as one of its typical features. That detail is worth sitting with for a moment. The fact that things improve after sun exposure in summer suggests the lack of it in winter is part of the problem, alongside the obvious cold.

None of this means every bit of flaking is a medical condition. Dandruff ranges from a light scatter of flakes most people barely notice to something red, sore and genuinely uncomfortable. What changes with the seasons is where you sit on that scale, and winter tends to push people further along it.

## What is actually going on with your scalp

The honest answer from dermatology is that the full picture is not completely worked out. [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/seborrhoeic-dermatitis) is upfront about this: the aetiology is not completely understood. What is understood points mainly to a yeast that lives harmlessly on everyone's skin.

Both sources describe the same mechanism in slightly different language. The [British Association of Dermatologists](https://cdn.bad.org.uk/uploads/2021/12/29200252/Seborrhoeic-Dermatitis-Update-April-2018-Lay-reviewed-April-2018.pdf) explains that 
seborrhoeic dermatitis is triggered by an overgrowth of a harmless yeast called Malassezia that lives on the skin, or an over-reaction by the skin's immune system to this yeast
, and is careful to add that 
these are not the same as the yeasts that cause thrush or those that are present in foods
. DermNet adds the biochemistry behind that overgrowth, noting that the lipases and phospholipases produced by Malassezia, a saprophyte of normal skin, cleave free fatty acids from triglycerides present in sebum, which may induce inflammation.

So the yeast is always there. What seems to shift with the seasons is how the skin responds to it, and how much sebum is around for it to work with. Add the obvious winter changes, colder air outside, hot water and central heating indoors, less daylight, and you have a scalp environment that behaves differently to how it does in June. The seasonal pattern itself is well established even though the precise trigger is not fully mapped out, and 
tiredness and stress can sometimes trigger a flare
 too, which is exactly the combination a lot of people are carrying into the darker months.

## Dandruff, seborrhoeic dermatitis, or something else?

These two get used almost interchangeably, and there is a reason for that. [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/seborrhoeic-dermatitis) describes ordinary dandruff as an uninflamed form of seborrhoeic dermatitis on the scalp, presenting as diffuse bran-like scaly patches within hair-bearing areas without underlying erythema, and it may be asymptomatic or mildly pruritic. In plain terms, dandruff is the milder end of the same condition, flaking without redness, and often without much itch at all.

Seborrhoeic dermatitis itself goes further. The [British Association of Dermatologists' leaflet](https://cdn.bad.org.uk/uploads/2021/12/29200252/Seborrhoeic-Dermatitis-Update-April-2018-Lay-reviewed-April-2018.pdf) describes it as a complaint where 
symptoms seem to vary from person to person, and affected areas can be itchy, sore and sensitive, with flaking skin that can be bothersome and embarrassing
. It is genuinely common. The same leaflet puts the numbers at 
about 4% of the population overall, with dandruff, which is mild seborrhoeic dermatitis of the scalp, affecting almost half of all adults
.

The [NHS dandruff page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dandruff/) is useful for working out whether what you have is likely to be simple dandruff or something that needs a proper look. It lists seborrhoeic dermatitis alongside other possible causes of flaking, including ringworm, eczema, contact dermatitis and psoriasis, each with a slightly different appearance, and makes one point clearly worth repeating: 
dandruff is not caused by poor hygiene, although it may be more obvious if you do not wash your hair regularly
.

If your scalp is just a bit flakier than usual with no redness or soreness, that is almost certainly dandruff behaving the way dandruff behaves in winter. If it is red, sore, weeping, spreading to your eyebrows or the sides of your nose, or genuinely uncomfortable, that is the point to stop guessing and get it looked at properly, which we will come back to.

## Why this matters more if you wear a hair system

A hair system base sits against the scalp for an extended period between visits, which is exactly why we build regular [hair system maintenance](/hair-system-maintenance) into how the service works. That rhythm, visiting every few weeks rather than managing everything yourself at home, exists because the skin underneath needs attention as much as the hairpiece does.

Winter is when that attention matters most. A scalp that is drier, flakier or more irritated than usual is going to feel that irritation more, not less, when there is a base attached to it. It is also harder to self-assess your own scalp condition under a system than it would be on bare skin, simply because you cannot see or easily feel what is happening underneath.

This is not a reason to panic about your system, and it is not something we can diagnose for you sight unseen. It is a reason to treat your regular maintenance visit as a genuine checkpoint rather than just a styling appointment, and to come in sooner than your usual schedule if something under the base feels different to normal.

## Keeping your scalp and hair system base comfortable through winter

The practical advice from dermatology sources is consistent and unglamorous, which is usually a good sign that it is sound rather than a fad.

- **Use a medicated shampoo if flaking has increased.** The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dandruff/) advises looking for a shampoo containing 
one or more of ketoconazole, coal tar, or salicylic acid, used for up to four weeks to see if your dandruff gets better
. The [British Association of Dermatologists](https://cdn.bad.org.uk/uploads/2021/12/29200252/Seborrhoeic-Dermatitis-Update-April-2018-Lay-reviewed-April-2018.pdf) lists the same family of ingredients for scalp use, including 
zinc pyrithione, selenium sulphide or ketoconazole, used regularly
.
- **Give the product time to work before rinsing it off.** The [BAD leaflet](https://cdn.bad.org.uk/uploads/2021/12/29200252/Seborrhoeic-Dermatitis-Update-April-2018-Lay-reviewed-April-2018.pdf) is specific on this point: 
for best results, wash into the scalp, then wait five to ten minutes before rinsing
. Rinsing straight away is one of the most common reasons a medicated shampoo seems not to work.
- **Deal with thick scale separately if it builds up.** For more stubborn scaling, the same leaflet suggests 
applying a descaling preparation containing coconut oil and salicylic acid for several hours or overnight before shampooing
, acknowledging it can be messy but usually effective.
- **Keep a plain moisturiser in the routine once things settle.** The BAD leaflet notes that 
a plain moisturiser may help to reduce scaling and redness of the skin
 once the scalp has calmed down.
- **Do not stop treatment the moment it clears up.** Flaking has a habit of returning, and the leaflet's own advice once symptoms improve is to 
continue using an anti-fungal shampoo once a week to reduce the chance of the rash coming back
.
- **Keep the base itself clean on the schedule it needs.** If you wear a system, the scalp underneath still needs washing and the base still needs proper cleaning between fittings. Our guides on [hair system maintenance](/blog/hair-system-maintenance-guide) and [showering, swimming and the gym with a system](/blog/swimming-gym-showering-with-a-hair-system) cover how that routine works day to day, and the [base clean and reattach](/hair-system-maintenance/hair-system-base-clean-and-reattach) visit is specifically where we look at the skin as well as the hairpiece.

None of this is a cure. The [British Association of Dermatologists](https://cdn.bad.org.uk/uploads/2021/12/29200252/Seborrhoeic-Dermatitis-Update-April-2018-Lay-reviewed-April-2018.pdf) is direct about that: 
treatment can improve and sometimes clear seborrhoeic dermatitis, but there is no permanent cure and the complaint tends to come back when treatment is stopped
. The aim through winter is comfort and control, not a permanent fix, and that is a realistic goal rather than a disappointing one.

## When to see a GP, and when to come to us

We are not able to diagnose or treat a skin condition, and we would never pretend otherwise. If your scalp is doing anything beyond ordinary flaking, a GP is the right first call.

The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dandruff/) is specific about when that call is worth making. See a GP if 
symptoms last a long time or become painful, you still have dandruff symptoms after using an anti-dandruff shampoo for at least four weeks, your dandruff is bad or your scalp is very itchy, your scalp is red or swollen, or you have flaky, itchy patches on your face or other parts of your body, since the GP can check your scalp for skin conditions that could be causing it
. If anything about your scalp genuinely worries you, or a rash appears alongside other symptoms you are concerned about, treat a GP appointment as the sensible next step rather than something to put off.
